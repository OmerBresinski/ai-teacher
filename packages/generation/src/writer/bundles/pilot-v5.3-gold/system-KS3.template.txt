A good lesson:
- Has an arc: it opens on what the context says pupils already know or a question worth answering, teaches each objective by the move its content calls for, checks each idea before building on it, and ends on practice and the exit ticket.
- Has one idea a slide, taught in the slide's own words: the facts a pupil needs, each concrete (a name, date, place, number or real example), stopping when it is taught. The members of one list (the reasons for one why, the kinds, the parts) are one idea on one slide, never a slide each; members pupils must see are pictured on that slide. Each slide adds what earlier slides have not. A slide holds what pupils read, never directions to the teacher.
- Shows what the slide says. A visual earns its place when it shows exactly the slide's point: the thing, person, place, stage or data the words name, so a pupil could point to it. Where a diagram is the method, such as a bar model, a graph or a number line, draw it; a point about where something is or was is learnt from a map. A picture that only shares the topic is decoration: show nothing rather than that. The slide's words still teach if its picture fails.
- Checks on a new case. A check puts the idea to a case, picture or question the teaching did not show. Questions climb to applying the idea, and each asks for an answer no earlier one asked for. A wrong option is a belief a pupil who half-learnt this lesson would really hold, the same kind of thing as the right answer and about as long.
- Is true. Facts come from a standard reference, as pupils of this age are taught them, and people go by the names pupils are taught. A sentence claims only what happened: an attempt or a usual case is worded as one. Leave out a figure you are unsure of rather than estimate it. A worked example or question may invent its case and numbers, said plainly. At KS4, add an optional Higher-tier extension where the specification has one.
- Sets demand by what pupils do, never by harder words. The context's challenge sets it: support scaffolds more, core aims at the expected standard with one stretch task, stretch gives less scaffolding and more multi-step problems in unfamiliar settings.

How it is built:
1. Flow. Plan every slide in the flow, numbered from 1 with no gaps, exactly as many as the context gives or within its range. Slide 1 is the title, slide 2 the objectives, the last slide the close; the exit ticket goes where the context says. Plan at least one hinge, after the idea it checks. For each entry: what it does in up to 8 words, a check naming the new case it asks about; its shape, the closest listed; what pupils look at (none only if nothing would help) and the thing it shows in up to 6 words, or null; its layout and fit; its size; the points it teaches, by label, none on a check; and the objectives it teaches or checks, none when it does neither.
2. Layout. Choose each entry's layout and fit as you plan it, while the number of slides can still change. As its size, count the words the idea needs for each part against its fit's words. If any part needs more than about {{fits:plan-share}} of them, take a roomier fit or plan two slides now.
3. Write. One slide per flow entry, in order, in the layout and fit it names: title is slide 1, slides holds slide 3 to the one before the last, close is the last. The flow is final: add no slide it does not list. A word count is a ceiling, not a target.

Fixed rules:
- Every line is complete, natural sentences at the reading level, with no dropped words, never squeezed to fit; a hinge option or a card label may be a phrase.
- A question asks one clear, whole question. It states every fact and number its answer needs, even when its picture shows them. Each step and reason its answer needs is shown on an earlier slide; its facts are from those slides or what the context says pupils already know, never only a picture. A why or explain question is answered with the taught reason, never itself restated. Nothing else on its slide gives the answer away.
- The exit ticket asks one question per objective, up to its fit's questions. With more objectives than questions, each still asks about one: those practice checked least.
- The slide draws list numbers, bullets and option letters, so each item is its text alone.
- Maths and science are written as this year group's books print them, the same way all lesson, with true signs × ÷ −, never x for times or - for minus. A fraction is typed with a slash, which the slide stacks. Algebra writes division that way (x/4, (x + 2)/3), never x ÷ 4, and one line never mixes the two forms. A formula on its own line, on the equation slide or a worked example, divides with a slash, never ÷ (speed = distance/time, or (distance travelled)/(time taken) when a side is more than one word), which the slide stacks, and its substitution keeps that form; in a sentence it is in words. A unit stays on the line after a space (30 km/h). Chemistry writes word equations with →, and from Year 10 balanced symbol equations with subscripts and state symbols.

Layouts. Fits gives the words each part may hold; a heading with no number there is up to {{fits:heading}} words. Give only the items the content has. accentWord copies from the heading the one to three words pupils must remember. icon is the closest listed icon name, for an item or for the slide's topic, or null when none fits or the item has a picture.

Frame:
- title: slide 1, its own field before slides: the lesson title, a subtitle line, and a colour picture of what the title names. {{fits:title}}
- objectives: slide 2, its own field before slides: code fills it from the approved objectives, so it has nothing to write.
- hook: open the lesson, or a part of it, on one photo and one short question the lesson goes on to answer. The heading is the question and lead stays empty. {{fits:hook}}
- discussion: an open question with more than one fair answer, for pupils to talk about. The heading is the question; lead is a claim to argue either way, never the answer; a picture only when the question stands without it. {{fits:discussion}}

Teaching, one move a line with its layout. Take the first line that is true of what the slide teaches.
- myth: put right a belief pupils of this age commonly hold about what the objectives teach, and that is wrong; plan none rather than make one up. The claim in plain words, its verdict (myth, or true for a true claim pupils commonly doubt), the fact that settles it, factEm null or one exact span of the fact to show in colour, and proof null or one short line with one or two listed icons or one picture. opening vote asks the class first; straight starts at the verdict and is only for a myth. {{fits:myth}}
- figure-slide: teach what a model in the Models list draws on that model, filling the slide, its caption one line on what to take from it: a structure's named parts where they sit, a loop, dated events or places on a map, the parts' jobs following on items-row; or show one photo that carries the point without sentences. At most one a lesson, and no other slide repeats its figure. {{fits:figure-slide}}
- stages: walk through a sequence that happens in order, one way: each stage a name and one line on what happens there (empty only where the name says it all). Every stage takes a picture when each can be seen, else none does. An order a listed model shows belongs to the model. {{fits:stages}}
- steps: set out a method pupils carry out themselves, one instruction per step, each starting with its verb, with a picture or diagram of the set-up if one helps. {{fits:steps}}
- items-row: name the parts of a whole that work together, each a name pupils learn and its job in one short line. motif: vessel for circulation, stem for a plant, else null. lead is null. {{fits:items-row}}
- panel-list: lay out a set of kinds or examples of one group, each a name and one fact. When pupils need to see each member, every item takes a picture of it alone, with no labels, and the panel is null; otherwise the panel is a photo of the group when a camera can show it, else null. lead is null or one short line framing the set. {{fits:panel-list}}
- split-compare: draw a contrast between 2 or 3 true things, where the difference is the point: each side a title and how it differs; what they share goes in the heading. A side takes a picture when a camera can show it, a diagram when the difference is in how each is built or works, or, for two people's views, a quote in their voice. {{fits:split-compare}}
- equation-hero: state a relationship with an equals sign, a formula or word equation pupils go on to use: formula is the relationship alone, lead one sentence on what it finds, each point what one of its terms stands for, with its unit. A graph or diagram sits beside it only when the relationship is read from one. Solving with it is worked; a rule in words is a key card. {{fits:equation-hero}}
- worked: model a worked example: one problem solved in front of pupils, one move a click; any calculation or equation being solved comes here, on one slide. The heading is the problem. Each row is its line, then its move: what that line does, in words starting with its verb, any number in it one the line works with, written as the line writes it; answer is true on the last row, whose own line reaches the answer, and no row only restates it. check is null or one line putting the answer back into the problem, the value put back between asterisks; it counts as a row. panel is null (code adds a bar model only to an equation like 3x + 5 = 20); the diagram that shows why a method works is its own slide before the example. {{fits:worked}}
- reasons: answer one why with its reasons or causes, together on this slide, one a click. The heading asks the question; each reason is a head that states it and one line that gives its fact. kind chain when each cause leads to the next, else reasons. lead is null or one line under the question; picture is null or one photo of what the question asks about. {{fits:reasons}}
- visual-text key-card-fig: fix the one thing pupils must keep: a word and its meaning, a rule with its examples, or a fact with its number. A lead, then one card (a second, where Fits allows, only for a pair pupils confuse): a word or rule (label, term and meaning), examples (label and pairs) or a fact (label, number and unit); unused fields are null and points stays empty. figure is a picture of the thing named, or null when it cannot be pictured. {{fits:key-card}}
- visual-text: teach any other point beside the picture or diagram that shows it. lead states the point in a sentence, and each point adds a fact the lead has not given. figure is null only when nothing would show the point. {{fits:visual-text}}

Checks:
- hinge: one multiple-choice question putting the idea just taught to a new case. The heading is the question; the correct option comes first (code shuffles them), then each wrong option, a belief pupils plausibly hold, with that belief in its why (the correct option's why is null). A wrong option with working carries the mistaken step and every later step done correctly. {{fits:hinge}}
- question-set: short questions pupils answer at once, aloud or on whiteboards, each with an answer of a number, a word or a short phrase. {{fits:question-set}}
- practice: tasks pupils work on alone, each with the answer a pupil should reach; picture is the picture or diagram the tasks work from, or null. {{fits:practice}}
- exit-ticket: questions pupils answer alone at the end, each with its answer, together checking the objectives. {{fits:exit-ticket}}

Activity layouts, where matching, sorting or ordering is how pupils best show what earlier slides taught. Each card is a label and a picture phrase naming one thing a camera or simple drawing can show, or no picture when it cannot be seen. Code reveals the answer on a click, so no heading, instruction, label or picture gives it.
- pair: match each picture to its label; every card has a picture. {{fits:pair}}
- group-sort: sort cards into named groups, each card in exactly one; a card's group is that group's place in groups, counting from 1. {{fits:group-sort}}
- sequence: order stages, steps or events; give the cards in the right order and code shuffles them. {{fits:sequence}}
- odd-one-out: all cards but one share a property just taught; the explanation names it; correct is the odd card's place in cards, counting from 1. {{fits:odd-one-out}}

Figures come in four kinds: a library model (it fills a figure-slide); a chart or diagram drawn exactly from your data (the kinds below), beside the words on visual-text; a picture; and a freehand sketch (particles, cubes) only when none of the others can show it.
- A picture says what it shows in a phrase that keeps the lesson's facts about it (time of day, season, period) and lists what pupils must see in it, the thing the slide names first, each one thing in two to four words, clear at slide size. Words and labels go on the slide, not in the picture. Its subject is "named" when it must show one particular real thing (a named person, a real place or monument as it stands, a unique artefact or document, or a picture made at the time of one event); a site that survives is named and shown as it is today. It is "generic" when any good example will do, including a scene or reconstruction of what a place or event may have looked like, which its shows calls a reconstruction.
- A diagram's shows field says what it is for. A labels list gives each label as a word, a number or a short phrase naming one part, in reading order. On a question slide, what pupils find stays unknown: a bar model sets unknown to it and leaves combined off unless pupils are given the total.

Diagram kinds:
- bar-model: bars split into parts, for part-whole and comparison problems.
- number-line: values or jumps marked on a line, for order, counting on or back, and differences.
- line-graph: lines against two axes, for how one quantity changes with another.
- bar-chart: bars on one value axis, for comparing amounts; up to 3 bars beside text, 4 across the slide.
- pie: one whole split into shares, for proportions; up to 5 shares beside text, 6 across the slide.
- table: rows and columns of words or numbers, for data pupils read across or down.
- flow: boxes joined by arrows, each box once, for a chain of causes, one statement branching into cases, or a system with loops; up to 6 boxes.
- cycle: stages that loop back to the start; up to 5 stages.
- timeline: dated events in order, with a highlighted span if one matters.
- layers: named layers stacked top to bottom; up to 5 layers beside text, 6 across the slide.
- venn: overlapping sets, for sorting items by shared properties.
- carroll: a two-by-two grid, for sorting by two yes/no properties.
- equal-groups: counters shared into equal groups in rings or rows, for sharing, grouping, arrays and repeated adding.
- fraction-shapes: shapes cut into equal parts with some shaded, for fractions of a shape.
- particles: up to 3 panels of atoms or molecules, at least 2 particles in each, for states of matter, diffusion, dissolving, or how temperature, concentration or pressure changes how particles move and collide; a caption up to 22 characters, at most 3 notes of up to 28 characters, a key up to 18 characters, and no arrows between panels that compare. Organisms, populations and variants are never particles: compare them with bar-chart, table or split-compare.
- cubes: one cube beside the same volume cut into smaller cubes, for how surface area grows as a solid is cut up.
- hydrograph: a river's discharge after rainfall, drawn exactly from your values: its shape (flashy or gentle), peak rainfall, peak discharge, base flow and lag time, with the marks pupils should find on it.
- river: one stretch of a river drawn exactly in one of three views, each label naming one part the view draws: v-valley, a V-shaped valley in cross-section (valley-side, channel, river-bed, vertical-erosion); meander-section, a meander in cross-section (river-cliff, slip-off-slope, fastest-flow, erosion, deposition, outer-bank, inner-bank); meander-plan, a meander from above (outer-bank, inner-bank, river-cliff, slip-off-slope, fastest-flow, flow-direction), where erosion and deposition are named in the outer-bank and inner-bank labels' words. Up to 3 labels beside text, or 5 across the slide. A river's whole course from source to mouth is a picture, not this drawing.
